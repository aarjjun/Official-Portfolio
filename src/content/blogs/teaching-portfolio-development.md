The moment a student realizes that code they typed on their local machine can be accessed by anyone, anywhere in the world, something clicks. That realization is what makes teaching web development so incredibly rewarding.

In March 2026, I organized and led a hands-on Study Jam focused entirely on Portfolio Development. Our audience consisted mostly of junior engineering students who understood basic syntax but had never deployed a real project.

### The Problem with Tutorials

Tutorial hell is a real place, and many students are stuck there. They watch hours of videos, copy the instructor's code line-by-line, and at the end, they possess a functioning app but zero understanding of its architecture. 

When I designed the curriculum for this session, I wanted to strip away the boilerplate. We didn't use massive frameworks right away. We started with the raw fundamentals: HTML semantics, CSS Flexbox and Grid, and vanilla JavaScript for interactivity. 

### Building in Public

I structured the session to be a "code-along" rather than a lecture. I put my editor on the projector and deliberately made mistakes. 

When a div refused to center, I didn't just quickly apply a fix. I opened Chrome DevTools, showed them the box model, and walked through the debugging process. I wanted to demystify the aura of the "perfect programmer." Developers make mistakes constantly; the skill lies in knowing how to read the console to fix them.

By the end of the three-hour session, over 50 students had built and deployed a responsive, personal landing page. 

### The Value of a Portfolio

I emphasized to the attendees that a portfolio is more than a digital resume. It is a canvas for your technical identity. It is proof of work. 

In a competitive job market, an employer can look at a thousand transcripts that all look identical. A custom-built portfolio, however, shows that you care about user experience, that you understand deployment pipelines, and that you have the initiative to build something outside of your assigned coursework.

Teaching this session reminded me why I love developer advocacy and mentoring. Knowledge is only powerful when it is shared.
